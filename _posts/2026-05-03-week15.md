From the presentations, many people are making solid contributions to their projects. Different groups are working on different projects, but they have encountered similar problems. Some groups found that maintainers were inactive or not very responsive, which made it difficult to get feedback or approval for their ideas. Almost every team found large and complicated codebases challenging. Many groups also mentioned that they had difficulty finding a suitable issue to work on, because beginner friendly issues were often taken very quickly, especially in large and active projects.

The lesson I resonated with the most is that before starting to work on an issue, it is important to check whether someone else has already claimed it. In open source projects, multiple people may be interested in the same issue, so claiming an issue or communicating clearly in the issue thread can help avoid duplicated work.

These presentations also made me reflect on the differences between our project and larger, more active open source projects. Our project is relatively small and quiet. There have not been any new issues posted since the semester began, so I had to scroll through older and older issues to find something suitable. In contrast, projects like pandas or p5.js have many new issues being posted, claimed, and solved very quickly. This can be both an advantage and a disadvantage. On one hand, active projects provide more opportunities and clearer examples of how people contribute. On the other hand, because many contributors are watching the same issues, beginner-friendly tasks can disappear very quickly.
